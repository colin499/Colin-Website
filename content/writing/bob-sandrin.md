At 8:00 AM, Bob Sandrin woke as usual on the silliest day of the year.  Good Friday wasn’t what it used to be.  Frankly, nobody really knew why people *celebrated* Good Friday.  All anyone knew was that upon waking up, there would be a haze in the sky.  An apparently non-poisonous haze.  But a haze nonetheless.  The green and yellow gaseous cloud would, without compromise, roll in overnight, park its gassiness right over the city of Gonsbury Massachusetts every Good Friday.  This haze tended to make people feel, well, silly.  Not silly “ha-ha” but more like silly “hee-hee”.  People ironically tended to get up to no good on Good Friday.  They uncannily claimed it was all in good fun but invariably, someone would end the day with their feelings hurt ,an immense strain in their marriage or at least a medical bill that is not covered by insurance.

Bob had made it through fifty-five Good Fridays and intended on seeing fifty-five more. God willing.  His wife, Marnie Sandrin, had been up since 4:30 AM.  Just as the haze invariably rolled its gassiness into the city of Gonson Massachusetts every Good Friday, Marnie Sandrin would spend the two weeks prior to Good Friday and the concurrent haze arrival in a state of manic preparation.  The canned food from last year’s haze would have another mountain of new cans stacked behind it.  The shudders on the window would be repaired, repainted, rehinged and rehung.  The twins, Brian and Benjamin, against their and their father’s wishes, would spend these energetic two weeks in home-school taught entirely by Marnie.  She outfitted a room in their house to look exactly like the classroom at the schoolhouse.  She made sure that every detail from their real classroom was in the home made classroom so that Brian and Benjamin wouldn’t suffer from any discontinuity of their education.  She’s not a monster Marnie!  She loves her boys and would do anything for them.

The family dog, Heffer, a Boston terrier, would not be allowed outside those two weeks.  Marnie had outfitted a special little room for him in the sunroom at the back of the house.  She filled it with tennis balls and cutouts of Malteses and chihuahuas so as to not make Heffer feel intimidated during his time indoors.  There was fresh wet food on a timer.  Every six hours and fifteen minutes, a fresh can of mostly beef dog food would crash down from a tube-like delivery system where it was spun next to a blade, flipped upside down and shaken vigorously by a robotic arm that Marnie had Bob create.  It was for the good of the family!  The food would inevitably fly elsewhere than the bowl, but Heffer paid no mind.  He lapped up all the chunks and made the clean up after the two week Good Friday preparation considerably easy.  He even minded where he would use the bathroom.  Marnie had built a doggy-toilet in the far corner of the room that for the rest of the year was covered by a wicker couch but during these two weeks, would be where Heffer would take care of his duties as dog.
